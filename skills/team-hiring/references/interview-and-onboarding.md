# The interview, and the first two weeks

## What the interview is actually for

One question: will this person hit the number every day without being chased.

Everything else, the resume, the years of experience, the polish, is weaker
evidence than how they react when you say the number out loud.

## Run it in this order

**1. State the expectation in the first three minutes.**

Say it plainly, as a fact rather than a question:

> "The daily expectation is 150 dials minimum, and at least two qualified leads
> passed off. Most of the team runs closer to 200."

Then stop talking.

**2. Watch what happens next.**

This is the highest-signal moment in the entire process and it costs nothing.

- Hesitation, a flinch, a pause that goes too long: pass.
- Immediately negotiating the number down: pass.
- Explaining why that number is unrealistic: pass, even when they are right,
  because they are telling you what week three sounds like.
- Asking how the number is measured, or what counts as a qualified lead: keep
  going. That is someone planning to hit it.
- Telling you they have run higher: keep going, then verify.

You are not being harsh. You are declining to hire someone into a job they have
just told you they do not want.

**3. Ask about the last role, specifically.**

Vague answers about responsibilities are worth nothing. Ask for numbers:

- What was your daily target, and did you hit it?
- What happened on a day you missed it?
- Walk me through yesterday at your last job, hour by hour.

The hour-by-hour question is the hardest to fake. Someone who genuinely did the
work answers it in concrete detail without effort.

**4. Test the actual skill, briefly.**

- Prospector: run a two minute roleplay. You are a mildly annoyed homeowner.
  You are listening for tone and recovery, not for a perfect script.
- Data manager: send a small messy list and ask them to clean it and say what
  they changed. Thirty minutes of their time, and it settles the question.
- Lead manager: give them a lead with a stated objection and ask what their
  next three touches are and when.

**5. Confirm the setup.**

If they did not send the screen recording with the application, get it before an
offer. Internet, machine, spoken English, and their ability to follow a simple
instruction, all in ninety seconds.

**6. Restate the number before the offer.**

Same number, third time. Then make the offer.

## Shortlist discipline

Interview several people per role even when the first is good, and keep notes
on the ones you reject and why. Two reasons: it calibrates you on what the
market actually offers at your price, and the rejected shortlist is who you call
first when the hire does not work out or when you split the role later.

Use the candidate-intake skill to keep the scored list rather than doing this in
your head.

## Onboarding: the first two weeks

A person who does not know their tasks and does not know where the procedures
live will look like a bad hire. Almost every time, that is a process failure
that was already in place before they arrived.

Have these four things ready before their start date.

**1. Mission, vision and core values, taught rather than emailed.**

Sit with them and go through it. People who are handed a document and told to
read it do not absorb it, and this is the part that decides whether someone
behaves well when nobody is watching.

**2. Their written daily, weekly and monthly task list.**

From `roles-and-kpis.md`, adapted to your operation. They should be able to
recite it by the end of week one. If you cannot hand this over, do not start
them yet.

**3. SOP access, and the habit of using it.**

Where procedures live and how to open them. Most operations have neither the
procedures nor a place to keep them, and it shows up as inconsistency the owner
blames on the hire.

Use the playbook-creator skill to turn a screen recording of the work into a
written SOP. Recording yourself doing the task once and converting it is far
faster than writing procedures from scratch, and it captures the small steps you
would forget to mention.

**4. Their North Star KPI and the review cadence.**

The number, how it is measured, and when it gets looked at. Set on day one, not
introduced in week four when something is already going wrong.

## Week one

Day 1: values, role, task list, tools, KPI. No production work.
Days 2 to 3: shadow the work. They watch, then narrate what you are doing.
Days 4 to 5: they do the work, you watch. Correct in the moment.
End of week: they walk you through their task list from memory.

## Week two

They run the role at reduced volume with a daily check-in. Raise to full target
by the end of the week. Keep the daily check-in for the first month, then move
to weekly.

## The ongoing rhythm

- **Daily huddle**, short. What happened yesterday, what today, anything blocked.
- **Weekly one on one** per person, against their number.
- **Monthly all hands**, whole company, each division says what it is working on.
- **Annual meeting**, blocked out properly, covering the year's plan and real
  education about the business. People who understand what the company is
  actually doing make better decisions when nobody is watching.

An hour of training does not excuse a missed daily number. A prospector who owes
150 dials still owes 150 dials on a day with a one hour session in it. Say that
during onboarding rather than discovering the disagreement later.
