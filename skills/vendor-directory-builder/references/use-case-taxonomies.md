# Use-Case Taxonomies: cover the whole job, not just the obvious trades

The point of a taxonomy is to make gap analysis (Phase 3) systematic: you compare what you
sourced against the full set of roles the use case needs, so you don't hand over a
"contractor list" that's missing the septic guy who kills the deal. Pick the taxonomy that
matches the user's use case, or build one with the method at the bottom.

## How to use these

In Phase 0 the user tells you the use case. Load the matching taxonomy below and treat it
as your coverage checklist. In Phase 3, walk the checklist: for any category with no solid
market-serving option, fill it from public records. Also run the **niche layer** prompts:
that's where the highest-value, least-obvious additions come from.

---

## Real-estate: full house-flip / rehab crew

The trades that touch ~90% of a flip:

- General contractor / whole-house rehab · Handyman · Plumbing · Electrical · HVAC ·
  Roofing · Foundation / waterproofing · Flooring · Painting · Drywall · Finish carpentry /
  cabinets · Decks.

Specialty trades a flip eventually needs (often thin in a general group, so source publicly):

- **Septic** (inspect/pump/repair): critical in rural/secondary counties; a failed field
  is a deal-killer. **Termite / WDO**: many closings need a termite letter from a
  state-chartered inspector. **Countertops** (stone fab + install): kitchens drive resale.
  **Cabinets**: a cheap stock/RTA source is different from custom carpentry. **Dumpster /
  junk removal / cleanout**: every rehab's first and last step. **Landscaping / tree**:
  curb appeal + distressed-lot tree work.

The niche / underground layer:

- **Excavation / underground utility / new water line**: trenching, directional boring,
  tapping the main. This is a three-party job (utility + excavator + plumber).
- **The regulatory/utility bodies that gate the work**: the water **utility district** that
  owns the main and sets tap fees (and whether it's a short- vs long-side tap, which decides
  who pays for the road bore); the permit office; the codes department. Put these in a
  reference tab. They're not vendors, but they control cost and schedule.

Usually don't bother chasing (a GC/roofer absorbs them): garage doors, gutters, siding,
windows, insulation.

## Real-estate: rental turnover / maintenance bench

Lighter and faster than a rehab crew: the goal is quick make-readies and reliable
on-call fixes:

- Handyman (the workhorse) · Plumbing · Electrical · HVAC · Flooring (labor, buy-your-own
  material) · Paint/drywall make-ready · Turnover cleaning · Landscaping/lawn · Locksmith/
  rekey · Appliance repair · Pest control · Junk removal.

## Real-estate: new construction / ground-up

Adds the earlier-stage and inspected trades:

- Surveyor · Excavation/site work/grading · Foundation/concrete · Framing · Utility
  service installs (water/sewer/electric/gas) · Rough-in trades (plumbing/electrical/HVAC)
  · Insulation · Roofing · Exterior (siding/windows/doors) · Drywall · Finish trades ·
  Flooring · Cabinets/countertops · Landscaping/final grade · plus the permit office and
  utility districts.

## General local-vendor sourcing (non-construction)

The skill isn't limited to trades. For any "find good local vendors for X" task, build the
taxonomy from the **stages or components of the job**. Examples:

- **Event/wedding vendors:** venue, catering, photography, florals, music/DJ, rentals,
  officiant, hair/makeup, transportation.
- **Small-business services:** bookkeeper/CPA, attorney, insurance broker, IT/MSP,
  marketing/web, commercial cleaning, signage/print.
- **Property management vendors:** the rental-turnover bench above plus a leasing photographer,
  a make-ready cleaner, and a 24/7 emergency plumber/electrician.

For non-licensed vendors, verification leans on verified reviews + business registration +
references rather than a license board, but the "does it serve the market, is it real, who
vouches for it, what's the honest confidence" structure is identical.

## How to build a taxonomy for a use case that isn't listed

1. **Decompose the job into stages or components.** What has to happen, start to finish, for
   this use case to be done? Each stage that a *different specialist* handles is a category.
2. **Separate the obvious from the specialty.** The obvious categories the user already
   knows; the specialty ones are where directories are usually thin and where you add the
   most value.
3. **Find the niche / gatekeeper layer.** Ask: *what hidden specialists, regulators, or
   utilities can block or blow up this job?* For construction it's septic/utility districts/
   permits; for events it's the venue's approved-vendor list and permits; for professional
   services it's licensing/compliance bodies. These belong in a reference tab.
4. **Confirm the taxonomy with the user** if the use case is unusual, then use it as the
   Phase 3 coverage checklist.

The test of a good taxonomy: if the user staffed the whole job only from your directory,
nothing would be missing at the moment they need it.
